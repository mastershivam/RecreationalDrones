This guide follows the Federal Aviation Administration's (FAA) own drone pages, read on 17 October 2026. Check
the FAA for any change.

There are two sets of rules. **Recreational** flying (purely for fun) is covered by the "Exception for Limited
Recreational Operations". Everything else, including most flying for work, falls under **Part 107**, the
Small UAS Rule. The FAA says that if you are not sure which applies, assume Part 107.

## Registration

Register at the FAA's [DroneZone](https://faadronezone-access.faa.gov/).

- A drone of **250 g (0.55 lb) or more** must be registered. The FAA's page words the boundary two ways ("0.55 pounds
  or less" is exempt, and "250 grams or more" must register), so a drone weighing exactly 250 g is unclear.
- A drone under 250 g that is flown **only for fun** does not need registration.
- A recreational registration costs **$5**, covers all your drones, and lasts **3 years**.
- A Part 107 registration costs **$5 per drone** and lasts 3 years.
- A registration cannot be moved between recreational and Part 107.
- You need a physical and mailing address, an email address, a phone number, the make and model, the Remote ID serial
  number (if the drone has one) and a credit or debit card.
- Carry the registration certificate, on paper or on a phone, when you fly, and put the registration number on the
  outside of the drone.

The registration page says an owner must be 13 or older (a person 13 or older can register for a younger owner) and be
a US citizen or legal permanent resident. For a foreign operator, the FAA says its certificate "is a recognition of
ownership", not a US aircraft registration.

## Recreational flyers

To fly for fun, the FAA lists these rules:

1. **Pass TRUST**, the Recreational UAS Safety Test, and carry proof. It is free and online, run by FAA-approved test
   administrators. If you lose your certificate you must take the test again.
2. **Follow the safety guidelines of an FAA-recognized community-based organization** (CBO).
3. Keep the drone within **visual line of sight**, or use a visual observer next to you who can talk to you directly.
4. Give way to other aircraft.
5. In controlled airspace (Class B, C, D and surface-level Class E at an airport), fly only with **prior FAA
   authorization**, through LAANC or DroneZone, and no higher than the altitude it gives.
6. In uncontrolled (Class G) airspace, fly at or below **400 feet (about 120 m)**.

The FAA's B4UFLY service, offered through approved apps, shows airspace, airports, national parks, critical
infrastructure and temporary flight restrictions on a map.

## Remote ID

A drone that must be registered, or is registered, must broadcast Remote ID (since 16 September 2023). You can:

- fly a drone with built-in Remote ID,
- attach a Remote ID broadcast module (you must see your drone at all times), or
- fly inside an FAA-recognized identification area (FRIA) with no Remote ID equipment, within sight.

## Flying for work (Part 107)

You need a **Remote Pilot Certificate**. The FAA says you must be at least **16**, read, speak, write and understand
English, and be fit to fly. A first-time pilot passes the "Unmanned Aircraft General – Small" knowledge test at an
FAA-approved testing centre, applies through IACRA, and passes a TSA security check. The certificate needs a free online
recurrent course every 24 months.

## Visitors from abroad

The FAA's page for international operators says:

- Foreign drones must follow Remote ID. If your foreign drone has Remote ID and is registered in your home country,
  you must send the FAA a **Notice of Identification** through DroneZone before you fly.
- If your drone is not registered abroad or has no Remote ID, you can only fly it inside a FRIA. Follow the DroneZone
  registration process to get a document showing ownership.
- The FAA does **not recognise a foreign remote pilot certificate**. To fly for work, either pass the FAA test (all
  testing centres are in the US) or fly under the direct supervision of a US-certified pilot.

## Not covered yet

- **Insurance.** Not mentioned on the pages we read.
- **State and local rules.** Many states, cities and parks have their own drone rules. We have not read any.
- **National parks and other banned places.** The FAA names stadiums, airports, restricted airspace, Washington DC
  and emergency scenes as restrictions, but we have not listed them. Check B4UFLY.
- **Temporary flight restrictions** change within hours. Check the FAA before you fly.
- **Penalties.** The FAA says breaking the rules may bring enforcement action and, for not registering, civil and
  criminal penalties, but we have not read the amounts.
- **Minimum age for recreational flying.** The pages we read give none.
- **Airport distances.** Controlled airspace around an airport has no single radius, so the map shows no airport
